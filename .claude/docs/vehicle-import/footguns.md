# Vehicle Import Footguns

## 1. Showing up at the Wrong Port

**Problem**: Not every CBSA port handles vehicle export. Even within designated ports, vehicle-export staffing is sometimes M-F only. Drive 8 hours to a port that can't process you, get turned around.

**Fix**: Call the chosen CBSA port directly the week before. Confirm vehicle export is handled, hours of operation, and expected wait. For non-US destinations, the CERS declaration commits you to a specific port — pick the right one before filing. For US-bound exports (documentation-on-request), the port is still important to confirm, but there's no CERS filing to amend.

## 2. Following Stale Guidance That Requires CERS for a US-Bound Vehicle

**Problem**: Prior versions of CBSA Memorandum D20-1-1, and older doc bundles built from them, described a "vehicle carve-out" that required a CERS export declaration (with a 72-hour advance notice) for all used self-propelled vehicles regardless of destination. The **October 2024 revision** of D20-1-1 (ISSN 2369-2391, modified 2024-10-21) superseded that rule. Under paragraph 20, a vehicle permanently exported to the **United States** requires only that documentation identifying the VIN be presented to CBSA upon request — no CERS declaration is filed.

**Fix**: For AB → WA or any Canada → US vehicle export, the procedure is documentation-on-request only. Do not file CERS; do have the registration certificate, Bill of Sale, PPR search, and photo ID available at the CBSA booth. Carry the D20-1-1 ¶ 20 binder reference (`12`) to present if a booth officer questions the no-CERS path.

> The CERS 72-hour rule still applies for vehicle exports to countries **other than** the United States. The rule is destination-specific, not globally abolished.

## 3. Driving to CBP Before CBSA Releases the Vehicle

**Problem**: A driver who skips the CBSA export booth and goes straight to the US side will be sent back. CBP cannot process an import without evidence that CBSA released the vehicle.

**Fix**: Always do CBSA first. Stop at the Canadian export booth, get the registration stamped/surrendered, **then** drive to CBP.

## 4. Forgetting the Build Date — Showing Up With Just "1968"

**Problem**: The 25-year FMVSS exemption is calculated by build date, not model year. CBP wants to see the build plate. If the build plate is missing or unreadable, the user falls back on alternative evidence (PHS report, dealer records).

**Fix**: Photograph the build plate and cowl tag before leaving Alberta. Carry the PHS report or equivalent.

## 4a. Pre-1981 VIN Format Breaks Modern History-Report Lookups

**Problem**: GM (and most North American manufacturers) used VINs of varying formats and lengths before the **1981 ISO 17-character VIN standard**. A 1968 GM vehicle, for example, has a **13-character VIN** (e.g., `223378U134833`). Carfax Canada / CarProof, AutoCheck, and most modern vehicle-history services validate input against the 17-character format and reject pre-1981 VINs as malformed. The buyer cannot get a standard history report.

**Fix**: Don't promise a Carfax-equivalent in your timeline for a pre-1981 vehicle. Substitutes:
1. **Provincial PPR lien search** (Alberta, BC, Ontario, etc.) — works on any VIN length, returns liens.
2. **Ask the seller for any prior ownership documents they retained** — old bills of sale, prior registration certificates, original sales paperwork.
3. **Marque-specific build records** (PHS for Pontiac; Marti Report for Ford; GM Heritage Center; etc.) — factory build records, not history reports, but they confirm originality and assembly.
4. **NICB VINCheck** (US, free, vincheck.nicb.org) — limited to theft/total-loss flags but accepts older VINs.

## 5. Assuming USMCA = Duty-Free

**Problem**: USMCA (formerly NAFTA) duty-free treatment for Canada-origin vehicles requires the vehicle to qualify as Canada-origin under the rules of origin. Most classic American cars built in Canada (small list — e.g., some Camaros built at Oshawa) might qualify, but the vast majority of US-classic cars sold in Canada were originally built in the US, sold in the US, then later moved to Canada. USMCA is irrelevant; **9801** is the right argument.

**Fix**: Don't claim USMCA on a US-origin classic. Claim 9801.00.10 with US-origin documentation.

## 6. LS Swaps and the EPA Exemption

**Problem**: A 1968 vehicle with a 2010 LS swap is technically still ≥21 years old, but the engine is not original-configuration. EPA Form 3520-1 Code E requires "original equipment configuration." A clearly modernized vehicle invites a closer look. Most enforcement is light, but a strict CBP officer can demand evidence the swap doesn't undermine emissions standards.

**Fix**: For period-correct or original drivetrains, no issue. For LS-swapped or modernized cars, document the swap (when, by whom) and consider declaring the engine type accurately. If concerned, route through a customs broker familiar with collector cars.

## 7. Buying a Vehicle With an Active Lien

**Problem**: A Canadian-side lien (recorded in the provincial PPR/RDPRM/registry) prevents export. The seller may not know — or may know and not disclose. CBSA's lien check is sometimes informal; CBP will not catch this. The buyer ends up with a vehicle they can't title because the underlying secured party has rights.

**Fix**: Run the PPR/PPSA search yourself before the sale (CAD ~$10). Require the seller to produce a written lien release if any was previously recorded.

## 8. Bill of Sale in French Only (Quebec) or Without Notarization

**Problem**: French-only Bills of Sale are not always accepted at the US border without translation. Notarization, while not strictly required by regulation, is requested by some CBP officers — refusing is a discretionary issue.

**Fix**: Use English (or bilingual). Notarize. Costs $20-100 in CAD and removes a discretionary objection.

## 9. Forgetting State Use Tax in the Project Budget

**Problem**: A buyer who's done the federal-side math and budgeted for $0 (9801) plus $115 (MPF) gets a shock at the WA DOL when use tax of $2,000-3,000 is owed. State use tax is the largest single cost in most cross-border classic imports.

**Fix**: Always include state use tax in the cost projection. Confirm whether the user's state credits any taxes paid in Canada (most do not — Alberta has no PST, so there's nothing to credit anyway).

## 10. Not Having Insurance for the Drive Home

**Problem**: A foreign-titled vehicle without US insurance is unlawful to drive in any state. The Canadian seller's insurance terminates at sale. Buying a permanent policy without yet having a US title and registration is a chicken-and-egg problem.

**Fix**: Bind a **transit binder** or short-term policy with a carrier that writes for foreign-titled vehicles being driven to the home state. Hagerty, Grundy, and some independents handle this. See the insurance team's classic-collector + in-transit notes.

## 11. Trip Permit Issued After Crossing the Border

**Problem**: WA's 3-day trip permit must be obtained **before** driving on WA roads. Driving from the WA-side CBP booth to a DOL office to buy the permit is technically illegal (driving without registration). In practice, CBP officers will sometimes wave the user through with a verbal warning; a strict officer or WA State Patrol stop will not.

**Fix**: Buy the WA trip permit online or at a vendor on the US side near the border before leaving home for the trip — or have a friend buy it on the user's behalf. Some users meet a friend on the US side who hands them the permit at the booth.

## 12. Treating "25 Years" as Model Year, Not Build Date

**Problem**: A 2001 model-year vehicle could have been built in late 2000. Importing it in early 2026 — model year says 25, build date says 26 — is a borderline case. Strict reading of 49 CFR 591.5(i) is build date.

**Fix**: For any import where age is close to the 25-year line, work from the build date. For our 1968 Firebird, this is a non-issue — well over 50 years old.

## 13. Personal Importation vs. Commercial — Misunderstanding Which Rules Apply

**Problem**: Personal importation by an individual (one vehicle, for own use) is different from commercial importation by a dealer. The rules above (HS-7, EPA 3520-1, age exemptions) cover personal. A user who unknowingly buys through a dealer-arranged import or who plans to flip the car within 12 months may be in commercial territory.

**Fix**: Personal importation requires the user be the importer of record. If a broker or dealer is involved, confirm whether they are filing as agent on behalf of the user (still personal) or as importer (commercial). Reselling within 12 months has no automatic federal flag for cars but creates a question of whether the original importation was personal in good faith.
